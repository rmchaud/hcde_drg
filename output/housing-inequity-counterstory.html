The housing crisis is unequal and political

This page is a counter-visualization to county-average “trend lines” alone: it foregrounds who carries housing cost stress, where geography in our local files stops (counties—not neighborhoods), and what you must add next (race, tracts, vouchers) from regional and state sources.

Data scope (files in data/raw/): All five CSVs are ACS-derived housing cost burden tabulations at region or county level. They do not include census tracts, race/ethnicity, Section 8 / HCV rates, or MTC-style displacement indicators. The map below is a schematic county layout, not a georeferenced basemap. Neighborhood claims require additional downloads (see sources at bottom).
Author read: The sharpest inequality signal in these tables is income-stratified burden: households reporting under $10,000 in income are overwhelmingly paying more than half of that income on housing in every Bay Area county in the 2024 extract—while households at $100,000+ are not. That pattern is structural; maps of “hot markets” without poverty and race layers erase the people most exposed.

Callout — who is most affected (income, within these files): For households with reported income under $10,000, the share paying more than 30% of income on housing is roughly 91–100% across counties in 2024; the share paying more than 50% is typically in the high 80s to high 90s percent. At $100,000 or more, the same “>30%” shares fall to roughly 13–20%. That wedge is the ethical center of the story these spreadsheets support.
Callout — where “displacement” is not visible here: Declining Black or Latino population share in a given neighborhood, or rising median rent at the block level, cannot be read from county aggregates. Several counties show lower renter severe-burden shares in 2024 than in 2009 (e.g. San Francisco in this tabulation)—which does not disprove displacement pressure; it reflects composition, incomes, who remains a renter, and ACS methodology. Tract maps are required for spatial displacement claims.

3. Your research questions → data we have vs data to add

  • Race/ethnicity and highest burden: Not in local raw CSVs. Use Bay Area Equity Atlas — Housing burden and race (tract or city where available) plus ACS crosswalks.
  • Change over time by race: Requires longitudinal race × burden tables; see California Housing Cost Burden (state data lab; check geography and vintage).
  • Largest neighborhood demographic shifts: Needs tract population by race/ethnicity over time (e.g. NHGIS, Census DHC, or MTC Bay Area displacement / housing summary).
  • Section 8 / voucher coverage vs displacement risk: Not in these five files; HUD PIC, Housing Authority dashboards, or regional equity layers are the right join targets.
  • Spatial corridors for lower-income residents: County maps cannot answer this; tract-level income and rent burden choropleths (Equity Atlas, CHHS) can.
